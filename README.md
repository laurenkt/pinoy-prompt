# Pinoy Prompt

A small, phone-first web app for learning beginner Tagalog with spaced repetition. Install it to your home screen and it works offline.

**Try it:** https://laurenkt.github.io/pinoy-prompt/ (open on your phone, then "Add to Home Screen").

## How it works

- **Study**: one button, and it keeps going until you stop. Each step is picked as you go: due reviews first, weakest first (lowest estimated recall), then a word you know starting a new way of asking (below), then a new card, but only once nothing is due, fewer than 3 cards are still being learned, and the daily new-card rate (8 by default) allows. When you're caught up it says so, and points to Drills for more practice (or a few more new cards); Study itself only asks what the schedule wants. Until the next card is answered, Previous card above it shows the one before again as you left it (its answer, hook and audio), to look at but not answer again, so its review counts once; Drills have the same way back.
- **Question types**: each word is three cards, one for each way Study asks it, each with its own schedule: reading (see the Tagalog, give the meaning), listening (hear it, give the meaning) and recall (see the English, give the Tagalog). Knowing a word one way says little about the others, so a word can be weeks out when read and due tomorrow when recalled. A word starts with reading; once that has settled (remembered a day after it was met), listening and recall start too, without using the daily new-word rate. While a way is still being learned it's a multiple-choice check (pick the meaning, or pick the Tagalog); once learned it's self-rated, so a lucky guess can't push it weeks out. Guides are one card each. Saving a way is keyed `id:listen` and `id:recall` beside the word's own id, which stays reading's (`cardKey` in `src/deck.ts`). Drills mix in multiple choice both ways, listening, and speaking once the pronunciation model is on the device.
- **New words**: every new card shows the Tagalog, audio, a pronunciation guide, the meaning, a usage tip and a memory hook, followed by a quick multiple-choice check. Once nothing is due, "Learn something new" at the end of a session adds 5 beyond the daily rate.
- **Beyond words**: cards aren't only vocabulary. Guides (`src/guides.ts`) teach sentence patterns ("Gusto ko ng ___"), sounds (the "ng" at the start of a word), how to say things (questions go up at the end) and everyday know-how (calling people Kuya and Ate). Each is introduced with a plain-English explanation and examples you can hear, then checked with a gap to fill. Once learned, a pattern comes back as a fresh sentence to build from tiles or to say from the English, so the rule is practised rather than one sentence; other guides are recalled and self-rated. Guides share the same schedule and daily allowance, and are eased in: none before 8 cards (patterns not before 20), only once the cards they build on have been remembered a day later, and at most one in every 6 new cards (one in 4 after 100). Explanations avoid grammar terms.
- **Schedule**: SM-2 style (1 day, 3 days, then growing by each card's ease). A missed word comes back after a short gap in the same session.
- **Drills**: the one place for practice beyond the schedule, opened from Home or from Study once you're caught up ("Keep practising in Drills"). One tap starts an unscored round over every card you've met, words and guides alike, that mixes listening, reading, recall and (once the pronunciation model is on the device) speaking, and keeps going until you close it. Each card is drilled each way on its own: knowing a word when you read it says little about recalling it from the English, hearing it or saying it, so a round picks a card and a way to ask it together (`pickDrillCard`). Cards still settling in (met in the last three days and not yet remembered twice in Study, still being learned), and ways of asking missed this round or that the drills had to help with, get a tenth of the questions each, up to half the round, so words you've just met keep coming up however big your deck gets without one new word coming round every few questions; the rest go to the other cards. Within each part a question is picked at random, weighted toward the ones most in need: by that way's own schedule where Study keeps one (reading, listening, recall; speaking and a way not started yet go by reading's) (`drillWeight` in `src/session.ts`: likely to have faded, still being learned, met in the last week and not yet settled, often forgotten, off to a hard start in Study, answered Again or Hard before holding a week), shared between the ways a card can be asked, and above all by how that way has gone: missed this round, or needing help in drills (a way that needed help once comes up more than a word met yesterday). Every card keeps a base weight so the strong ones still turn up now and then; the last three cards asked sit out, every way. Guides are asked the way Study asks them (`src/screens/guide-question.ts`): a gap to fill counts as Read, a pattern's sentence to build from the English as Recall; they aren't heard or said. A small filter button in the round's header narrows it to the kinds you pick, from the next question on; the choice lasts until the app is closed. A right answer gets a short, quiet two-note chime (not with the sound off), and a word said back with it plays just after. Across the app, pressing a control makes a soft, short knock: a thock for the big chunky buttons, a lighter click for chips, pills, links, checkboxes and opening a section, unless the press plays a sound of its own, opens the microphone, or silent mode is on; Settings has a Tap sounds switch to turn it off (on iPhone app audio takes over the phone's, so a tap sound can pause music). Drills never change the review schedule. How well a card is known stays out of sight while it's asked, so it doesn't colour the answer: a tap anywhere on the card (not on its own buttons) turns it over, and another turns it back, for that question only. Its back (`src/screens/card-back.ts`) leads with a dial of how likely it is to come to mind now, and when Study asks it again; then three pictures explain it, sitting straight on the card, each under a plain title and a short line on how to read it: the slope that chance slides down from its last answer on, with a ball where it is now and a flag where Study asks it again; a staircase of strengths (Not yet, then Just met up to Long-term) with a tag for every way the card is asked standing on its step, the way asked now in saffron and labelled, and in words the ways the drills had to help with lately; and the gaps between reviews over its life as a skyline, each building as tall as how long Study waited to ask again, against a day, a week and a month (a forgotten card drops to the ground); and how many times Study has asked it that way (`reviews` on the card, counted from this build on; a card answered before then shows "at least", starting from its current run and the times it was forgotten, and keeps counting on from there, `reviewsAtLeast`), how many times it was forgotten, and how long since it was met. A way Study doesn't keep a schedule for (Speak, or one not started yet) is drawn from reading's, and says so. Listening steps up by itself: a word still being learned is picked from four meanings, and once it has settled in Study (remembered a day after it was met) you type its meaning instead, and an answer that is exactly the card's own wording (in any case, punctuation or spacing) is taken as soon as typing pauses; anything else, typos included, waits for Check. With an OpenAI key, a microphone button beside the box records the meaning said out loud (only when tapped, and never with the mic off) and OpenAI's speech-to-text (`gpt-4o-mini-transcribe`) turns it into text to check. That's judged on meaning, not spelling (`src/meaning.ts`): typos, contractions, digits for numbers and "a"/"the" don't matter, and with an OpenAI key (the one Chat uses) other wording is checked by a small model (`gpt-5.4-mini`), so "thanks a lot" counts for "Thank you very much". The meaning has to be in English: saying or typing the Tagalog back is never right, even though it means the same (the device catches the card said back, maybe with a "po", without asking OpenAI, and OpenAI is told the same). Without a key, or offline, only the on-device check runs; either way "I had it right" overrides a miss (except a misspelt recall answer, below), and "Show choices" goes back to picking for a word you're unsure of. Any drill answer that needed help (a wrong pick, a wrong typed answer, one picked after "Show choices", or a word said wrong in Speak before it was said right or moved past) brings that card back asked that same way much more often in later rounds, until it's got right unaided: each, up to three a way, is kept on the card in `help` (with when it last changed, `helpAt`, so a copy synced from another tab keeps the later count), which only Drills read. One right unaided takes one off, "I had it right" takes the miss back, and a typed answer that couldn't be checked (OpenAI unreachable) doesn't count. Recall steps up the same way, the other way round: a word still being learned has its Tagalog picked from four, and once it has settled you type the Tagalog from the English (or say it into the box, with a key or with the pronunciation model on the device), with the word played only once it's answered. Said Tagalog is heard by its sounds first: with the pronunciation model on the device, the recording is judged against the card's Tagalog the way Speak judges it (`judge` and `passes` in `src/phonetics.ts`), with every word said (`everyWordSaid`, so "Salamat" isn't taken for "Salamat po"), and one that passes is right without going anywhere. Speech-to-text writes words it knows, and wrote "kumusta" as "como esta", so only an answer that doesn't pass (or any, without the model) goes to OpenAI to be written out (without a key, one that doesn't pass is neither right nor wrong: the box asks for it again or typed), told to write it as said in Tagalog spelling, and that text is checked as a typed one is. The device takes the card's own wording or an accepted spelling, in any case or punctuation, with an added "po" (but not one left out of "Salamat po", which is what makes it polite). Typed, it has to be spelled right, since spelling it is part of learning it: the card's wording with a typo is wrong, says "Nearly: check the spelling." and offers no "I had it right". Said and written out by speech-to-text, a typo still counts. Other wordings go to OpenAI, told to check it as Tagalog, and when typed, to check its spelling too. Here the answer has to be in Tagalog: the English said back is never right (OpenAI is told so; the device leaves it to OpenAI, since it can't tell English from a word Tagalog borrows, like "traffic", or a number dictated as digits). This is different from Speak, which shows the Tagalog and checks how you say it on the device.
- **Home**: the one hub, with no tab bar. Big Study (showing how many cards are due) and Chat buttons, smaller Drills and Library buttons, then a chart of your memory: how firmly each card is stored over time (just met, days, weeks, long-term, read from its review interval), with how often you recall them (measured from your reviews over the last 30 days, or estimated from the schedule until there are enough). The chart carries on past today as a hatched area rising from the cards that have stuck towards the next milestone, named in its top corner with roughly how long it might take (Everyday basics, in about 11 months) and dated on the axis; drag into it to read the projection. The milestones are everyday basics at about 500 words, getting by on a trip at 1,000, casual conversation at 2,500 and talking about most things at 4,000. Like the rest of the chart they are counted in cards: each is turned from words into cards by how many cards the deck spends per Tagalog word it teaches (all its cards, three for each word or phrase and one for each guide, over the different words its words and phrases use, about three cards a word). It counts cards that have stuck (holding a week or more), not cards seen, and runs on from today at your pace over the last four weeks, bending the way your own history has on the phone: steady if you have kept meeting words at a steady rate, easing off if that has been slowing down (at most to words ∝ days^0.7). Tapping its name lists them all. The word counts are rounded from studies of English and other European languages, as there are none for Tagalog, so the app just calls them rough. A drag on the chart that starts up or down scrolls the page; one sideways looks through time, as does a tap, and another tap puts it back on today. A finger moving across it ticks softly at each day, with a light haptic tick where the phone allows (Android, or an iPhone on iOS 18 or later), unless Tap sounds is off. In the first two weeks too few cards have had a week to stick, so it shows an early guess instead, from every card met, called an early guess in the list. With no new cards sticking lately there is no forecast. There are no lessons to complete or streaks to keep. On Home, under the settings button, a split button sets **quiet mode**, for the tube or anywhere you can't talk or be heard: one half turns the mic off, the other the sound, each crossed out and teal while it's off, in any mix. With the mic off nothing asks you to speak (no Speak in Drills, no saying a typed answer). With the sound off no audio plays and nothing asks you to listen, so listening cards wait in Study until the sound is back on (a listening card on screen when the sound goes off is skipped, left as it was). With both off, every card is seen and answered by tapping or typing. A web app can't tell headphones from the phone speaker, so this is set by hand. Speaking steps never change the schedule. When a drill round that may ask you to speak starts, the phone asks for the microphone straight away, over a short holding screen (not with the mic off, and without the holding screen when the phone already allows it), rather than in the middle of a word to say; the app then keeps the microphone until you leave. Refusing it leaves speaking out for that visit. The same button is in the Study header and on Drills.
- **Library**: everything there is to learn, filterable by kind: words and phrases by topic (tappable for audio), then patterns, sounds, how to say things and know-how. Each word you know shows how firmly it is stored, by its weakest way met (read well but only just recalled shows as just recalled), and each topic's and guide group's heading is filled from the left with its cards by strength in the chart's colours, firmest first, the rest of the heading standing for cards not met yet.
- **Chat**: a spoken conversation with an AI in English, with the learner's Tagalog mixed in. See below.

New words come most useful first, whatever their topic, at the daily rate set in Settings: `LEARN_ORDER` in `src/deck.ts` ranks every card by how soon it helps in conversation (hello, thanks, yes/no and "I don't understand" first; "I'm on my way" and "now / later" early; "Traffic!" and the weekdays much later). The words phrases are built from get their own cards first: the little ones (`CORE_WORDS`: I, you, we, my, your, the, this, already, just, to, of, "ba", "po", "not") and the ones several phrases share (how, where is, to say, there is, want, need, can, time). Every word of a phrase that has a card comes before it in the order, and the phrase waits until those words have settled (remembered a day after they were met; `buildsOn` in `src/deck.ts`, used by `upcomingNewCards` in `src/session.ts`), with the next card in the order coming in meanwhile. So "Paano sabihin ito sa Tagalog?" comes a day or so after "paano", "sabihin", "ito" and "sa", and is one thing to learn rather than five. A phrase brings at most one word with no card of its own, learned in context (English words like "okay" aside); `src/deck.test.ts` checks both. Topics only group words on the Words screen, and a topic's intro shows before the first of its words a learner meets. The deck is weighted towards getting by quickly: greetings and politeness, meeting people, numbers, questions, the building blocks (I, you, my, the, this, already, there is, I need, can I), telling time, when things happen, travelling, places and directions, making plans, food and eating out, money and shopping, family, everyday phrases and small talk ("Kumain ka na ba?"), chat reactions, how you feel, doing words (eat, sleep, buy, wait), describing things, colours, the weather, phones and texting, and help in an emergency. Cards teach Tagalog the way people in Manila actually speak it, not the textbook version: "Uy!" and "Saan ka galing?" as hellos, "Wala 'yon" for you're welcome between friends, "Mauna na ako" to leave, the squeezed "Anong ...?" and "Konti", and English words where everyone uses them ("Anong number mo?"), with a know-how card on Taglish. Formal forms that are still worth recognising ("Magandang umaga", "Paalam", "Ikinagagalak kitang makilala") say so in their tip. Progress is kept by card id, so a card's id must never change once released (`src/deck.test.ts` checks this). A card can be reworded under the same id when it still means the same thing; its old wording goes in `accept`, so a learner who learned it that way is still right.

Progress is stored on the device. Nothing is sent to our server (there isn't one), and that includes your voice: speaking questions are checked on the device. The one exception is OpenAI, only once the learner adds their own key for Chat: Chat sends the conversation's audio, and a listening or recall drill sends a typed answer the device couldn't match, with the card it was for, and an answer said out loud after a tap on its microphone button (for recall, only one the device didn't pass as the card).

## Chat

Chat (called Talk in the code) is a spoken conversation in English with the Tagalog the learner knows mixed in, using OpenAI's GPT-Live (`gpt-live-1`) over WebRTC.

- **What gets mixed in** (`src/talk.ts`): every word the learner has met, grouped by how firmly it's stored, plus one word they haven't met yet (the next one in the chosen topic). The AI is asked to speak English, Taglish style, with only those words swapped in and no other Tagalog (unless the learner asks how to say something, which always gets a straight answer). The words are what it may use, not a list to get through: it says what it would naturally say and swaps in a word only where the sentence already needs that meaning, never steering the conversation to fit one (but using the Tagalog, not the English, wherever one does fit), and brings in the new word only if a moment for it comes up. Each sentence has a ceiling, not a target: at most one Tagalog word or phrase for a beginner, two or three past 20 words, and three or four past 60. So as words are learned, more of each conversation can turn Tagalog on its own. The instructions follow OpenAI's GPT-Live prompting guide, and the call opens with an English greeting sent as a developer message and then as commentary once the session starts.
- **On screen**: a full-screen WebGL backdrop played by both voices (`src/visualizer.ts`): a kaleidoscope tunnel that rushes forward with speech, oscilloscope rings drawing the raw waveforms (teal for the learner's microphone, saffron for the AI) and colour splits on sharp sounds. It reads the raw waveform from Web Audio analysers each frame, with an envelope that rises instantly, so it moves with the sound rather than after it. It lies dormant while the call connects (dark, colourless and deaf to the microphone, with one grey spark circling), so it never looks ready before it is. When OpenAI says the session has started it bursts into life: outlined bands of saffron, magenta and teal blast out from the middle bringing the colour with them, and the tunnel lurches forward. When the call ends or drops it powers down and stays dark behind the ended screen. With reduced motion the colour only fades in and out. Leaving the screen by any route (×, the back gesture) hangs up through `app.navigate`, and a call still connecting is given up at once. Over it, the live transcript of the call, which follows the newest words unless the learner scrolls back. The deck's Tagalog is underlined in its strength colour. Tapping one plays it and shows its meaning. The AI says the meaning of words met in the last day, and of the new word, aloud.
- **Practice, not a test**: no scores, and the review schedule is never touched. Mistakes aren't marked; the AI uses the right form in its reply instead.
- **The learner's own key** (`src/openai.ts`): Chat uses an OpenAI API key the learner pastes in. It's kept in `localStorage` apart from progress, so it never goes into a backup, and is sent only to `api.openai.com`. The browser sends its WebRTC offer straight to `POST /v1/live/sessions` with that key; there is no server of ours. Calls end after 10 minutes, and the call screen shows the running time and rough cost (GPT-Live is billed at about 5¢ a minute).

Why not "Sign in with ChatGPT": its self-serve route is for open-source apps running on the user's own computer, with a `127.0.0.1` callback, and its sign-in endpoints don't allow calls from web pages. The hosted-website route needs OpenAI's approval and a backend. Neither fits a static PWA, so Chat uses a pasted key instead.

## Saved progress

A learner's progress is the one thing the app can't afford to lose, so it is kept with some care:

- **Versioned.** Progress is saved in `localStorage` as `{ schema, rev, savedAt, progress }`. `src/progress-schema.ts` upgrades older copies one schema at a time, repairs damaged cards field by field instead of dropping them, and keeps fields it doesn't recognise, so an older build never strips data a newer one added. To change the schema, bump `SCHEMA`, add a migration and add a fixture under `src/test/fixtures/`; the tests fail until all three exist.
- **Two copies.** Every save is mirrored to IndexedDB. On start-up the newer copy wins, so a cleared or unreadable `localStorage`, or a write iOS lost by killing the app, is recovered from the mirror.
- **Saves never remove cards.** Each save re-reads what is stored and merges it in, keeping the more recently reviewed version of each card. Two open tabs can't overwrite each other, and a start-up that failed to read storage can't save an empty copy over real progress. Only Reset replaces progress, and it keeps the old copy under `pinoy-prompt:before-reset`. A copy that can't be read at all is set aside under `pinoy-prompt:unreadable`.
- **Splitting a word into its ways (schema 4).** Before schema 4 a word had one schedule for every way of asking it. The upgrade keeps that card as the word's reading card and gives a word already settled copies of its schedule as its listening and recall cards (marked `split`), so nothing is lost and nothing well known comes back as new; a word not yet settled starts the other ways as usual. A copy never overwrites a way that already has a card, and when progress from another tab, the mirror or a backup is merged, a way's real answer always beats a migrated copy, whatever its dates; answering a copy drops the mark. Drill help (`help`, `helpAt`) stays on the reading card.
- **Kept from eviction.** Once there is progress, the app asks the browser for persistent storage (`navigator.storage.persist()`).
- **App icon badge.** Installed to the Home Screen, Settings can show the number of cards due on the app's icon (off until switched on; on iPhone it needs notifications allowed, though the app never sends any). It's the count when the app was last opened or left, since a web app can't change its icon while it's closed without a push server, and iOS shows a notification with every push.
- **Backups.** Settings can save a backup file (through the share sheet on iPhone) and restore one. Restoring merges, so it never removes anything. On iPhone the Home Screen app and Safari keep separate storage, so a backup is how to move progress between them.

`src/store.test.ts` plays out the ways progress could be lost: unreadable or full storage, a wiped `localStorage`, a lost write, a hung IndexedDB, two tabs saving at once, upgrades from the first storage format, and resets. `src/progress-schema.test.ts` loads a fixture from every schema version and throws thousands of randomly damaged copies at the loader.

## Speaking questions

The **Speak** practice mode shows a word or phrase (without playing it or showing its sounds-like spelling, so it asks how the learner remembers it sounding rather than having them repeat it; both come once they've had a go), records the learner saying it, and coaches them on each sound: the letters they said well, the ones that were nearly there, the ones that were off, and a short tip for the most important fix (for example "ng is one sound, like the end of *sing*" or "Filipino r is a quick tap, like the tt in American *butter*").

How it works:

1. **Recording** (`src/recorder.ts`): the microphone is read at its native rate, shown live on a level meter, downsampled to 16 kHz, and stopped automatically after a short silence (or by tapping "I'm done"). To make that wait as short as it can be, the microphone (already held for the round) is switched on as the question comes up and its sound thrown away until the tap, so the take starts with sound already flowing. The screen asks the learner to speak only once the microphone is giving sound: until then it says "Getting the mic ready…", and then the prompt card turns magenta and the big button saffron, so it's plain the take has begun. If the microphone gives no sound at all, not even hiss (iOS can hand back a silent stream), it's opened afresh once, and if that's silent too the learner is told the microphone isn't working rather than that they weren't heard.
2. **Recognising sounds** (`src/fbank.ts`, `src/phone-model.ts`): the audio becomes 80-band log mel features, computed exactly as in training (checked against lhotse in `src/fbank.test.ts`), and goes through [ZIPA](https://github.com/lingjzhu/zipa) (Zhu et al., ACL 2025), a multilingual phone recogniser that outputs IPA sounds rather than words. We use the 64M-parameter `zipa-small-crctc` model, int8-quantised, run with onnxruntime-web in WebAssembly. Besides the sound it wrote, each sound keeps the others the recogniser nearly wrote in its place (at least 10% likely at some moment), as it often hesitates between close sounds like l and n. Model © its authors, [CC BY 4.0](https://huggingface.co/anyspeech/zipa-small-crctc-500k).
3. **Expected sounds** (`src/phonetics.ts`): Tagalog spelling is close to phonemic, so a rule set turns each card into IPA (ng → ŋ, r → ɾ, mga → maŋa, ts → tʃ, borrowed letters, optional glottal stops), with the everyday ways of saying a spelling allowed beside it: the vowel of an "iy" or "uw" after a consonant left out ("dyan", "pwede"), s, d, t and n blending with that y ("sha" for siya, "jan" for diyan, "chak" for tiyak, "nyo" for niyo), and a g before p, t, k or s said k.
4. **Comparing** (`src/phonetics.ts`): the two sound sequences are aligned with an edit distance weighted by phonetic features, so a near miss (aspirated p, o said as ou) costs less than a different sound. Tagalog allophones (o/ɔ, e/ɛ, r/ɾ) count as right, and so do the ways native speakers say a sound from region to region and in everyday speech: o and u, e and i swapped, an unstressed a relaxed towards "uh", r and d swapped, f and v said p and b, a voiced s, and k and g softened between vowels. A few count as nearly right (half a sound): l said as a tapped r, an English r (as in Taglish "number"), and "ng" heard as n at the start of a word, where the recogniser often hears even a native speaker's "ngayon" as "nayon". A sound the recogniser nearly wrote counts as heard (`runnersUp`). Added sounds count against the attempt (except a glottal catch or a released final consonant). A card can list other standard pronunciations with `accept: [...]` (Kumusta accepts Kamusta); the attempt is judged against the closest one.
5. **Coaching** (`src/coach.ts`): common English-speaker patterns get a specific tip; anything else gets "sounded like X".
6. **Passing** (`judge` and `passes` in `src/phonetics.ts`, used by `src/screens/speak.ts`): an attempt counts as right at 90% or more (`PASS`), where a sound said well scores 1, a nearly-there one ½, and an added sound counts against it. The sounds the recogniser writes down miss some it heard, above all a first p, t or k (Tagalog's are said without a puff of air, which it expects), so it also counts as right from 70% (`ROUGHLY`) when the recording is likely to be the word (`likelihood`, at least `LIKELY`): from the recogniser's odds at each moment, it lines the card's sounds (and the ways native speakers say them) up against the whole recording, as the recogniser itself aligns sounds, and compares that with what it thought most likely. A different word, even a sound away, is unlikely, so it still fails. The aim is clearly understood and fairly natural rather than native-perfect: a phrase passes with about one sound in ten off (one from ten sounds, two from twenty), as a listener still gets it in context, while a short word with a sound off (which can make it another word) gets another go. A right attempt moves on to the next word by itself after a couple of seconds, as a right multiple-choice answer does; tapping anything on the result (to play your attempt back, say) stops it.

7. **Stress** (`src/prosody.ts`, `src/stress.ts`, `stressTip` in `src/coach.ts`): for a single word of two or more syllables, Speak also checks which of its last two syllables (where Tagalog stress falls) was leaned on, against the capitals in the card's hint. The two vowels the recogniser heard there are measured from the recording: how long each lasted (to the next sound, or to where it drops 10 dB under its own peak, so the silent hold of a final p doesn't count), its pitch near its peak (YIN, in plain TypeScript, three quarters of the way up its voiced moments, so one moment read an octave out doesn't decide it) and its loudest moment. Finding pitch takes a moment, so nothing else is measured, and a vowel with no pitch (whispered) leaves stress unjudged. The two vowels are compared, pitch in semitones, and weighed together (`WEIGHTS`); when it's sure (`SURE`) the attempt leaned on the other syllable, the result card says which to lean on, spelled as in the hint, under the word: "Lean on LAH, not MAHT: higher and longer." It is a tip only: it never changes the score or whether the attempt passes, and when it isn't sure it says nothing. Phrases aren't judged, as the sentence's own rise and fall outweighs each word's stress there, and nor are the words whose own clips don't measure the way their hint says (`UNJUDGED`, mostly final-stress words the voices say gently), so a learner who says a card like its clips is never told otherwise. Pitch turned out to be the strongest cue in these recordings, ahead of length, which listeners lean on most in studies of Tagalog: a word said alone ends its phrase, so its last syllable is drawn out either way, and the recogniser times sounds as the moment it heard them rather than as spans. [The research behind it](https://claude.ai/artifact/WzB8t8jPy6t2cVc8wCLWWp) has the sources and measurements.

**The results screen** breaks the attempt down sound by sound (`src/screens/speak-result.ts`), and fits on one phone screen:

- The card becomes the result: the word with each letter coloured by how its sound came out. The whole word to play back, yours or the target, sits at the bottom above Next and Try again, under the thumb.

- The recording is drawn as a ribbon (`src/spectrogram.ts`, WebGL with a 2D canvas fallback): it swells with loudness, holds the pitch spectrum inside, and is outlined in each sound's verdict colour. A glowing playhead follows whatever is playing.
- Each sound is marked along the ribbon: the sound aimed for above, what was heard below, in its verdict colour. A sound that wasn't said gets a dashed slot where it should have been. Tap a mark to open that sound, or drag along the ribbon to open each sound in turn (ticking at each one, like the memory chart). The word above it works the same way by its letters: drag along it, or tap a letter; the open sound's letters are underlined.
- The open sound gets its tip, a closeness bar and a map of where it's made in the mouth (`src/sound-map.ts`): the vowel chart for vowels, a where × how grid for consonants. Your mark and the target sit on it with a path between them. Under it, a slider plays what you said (left of the middle) or what to aim for (right of it): hold it to hear that sound for as long as you hold, quiet near the middle and louder the further your thumb goes, while its mark on the map pulses. Let go and the thumb goes back to the middle. A sound said right, or one that wasn't said, gets a single button to hold instead, at a set, moderate volume.

The recogniser's output gives each sound a time (from the CTC frames), which is how the ribbon places it. The sounds the slider plays are made in code (`src/phone-synth.ts`), not cut from the learner's recording (a slice of one sound is too chopped to be useful) or from a native recording (which can't be held): a buzzing voice shaped by the mouth's resonances, with hiss for s and sh. Vowels and hissing sounds carry on while held; other consonants repeat with "a" after them, as "ma-ma-ma". Vowels are placed from the same chart positions the map draws, so two sounds far apart on the map sound far apart too.

The model is a one-time download of about 71 MB, fetched from Hugging Face only after the learner agrees, then kept in the browser's Cache Storage so it works offline. A download that's cut short is never kept. Once the model is on the device, the service worker also keeps the model's runtime (about 11 MB, listed in the build's `asset-manifest.json`) across app updates; learners who never use speaking don't download it. Mixed rounds include speaking only once it's on the device.

Why this approach: the browser's speech recogniser can only say whether it understood the word, which isn't coaching, and it isn't available at all in apps opened from an iPhone's home screen ([WebKit bug 225298](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=225298)). Comparing against reference audio would need recorded clips for every card. A phone recogniser hears what was actually said, works on any modern browser including iOS, and keeps the voice on the device.

Stress is checked the same way (`src/spoken.test.ts`, `src/stress.test.ts`): no card's own clip may draw a stress tip, `UNJUDGED` must list exactly the words whose clips don't all measure as their hint says, and of takes in the same voices said with the stress moved on purpose (kept as measurements in `src/test/fixtures/stress-takes.json`), at least half must draw the tip, and at most one in ten of those said the hint's way. Its weights were fitted on those clips and takes, so a learner's real slips may be caught less often than that. Measuring the clips also found 11 hints with the capitals on the wrong syllable (masarap, malamig, talaga and others), since fixed.

Tuning: the scoring is checked against every card's own clips (two native-sounding voices, at both speeds) on every test run: they must all pass, and hardly any may pass as a clearly different card (one the clip's card, said perfectly, would score under `ROUGHLY` as): a few short words the recogniser can't tell apart in these voices, like opo and apo, are allowed, up to one clip-and-card pair in 20,000. Words a sound or two apart (kanan and kanin) are decided by how likely the recording is each, and the recogniser can't always tell: about 8% of those pairs pass, and the test fails above 10% (`src/spoken.test.ts`, from what the recogniser heard in each, kept in `src/clip-sounds.json`). Before these checks 1004 of the 1528 clips failed. It was also checked against 200 sentences read by native speakers ([FLEURS](https://huggingface.co/datasets/google/fleurs) Filipino dev set): the words passing went from 72% to 83%. Earlier, it was tuned against 73 of those sentences ([FLEURS](https://huggingface.co/datasets/google/fleurs) Filipino dev set), so that natural Tagalog isn't marked wrong: "ng" taking the sound after it (m before p/b, n before t/d/s/l), the particle "ng" said "nang", "ay" said "e", long vowels, and the recogniser writing k as q. In that fast, connected speech about 80% of words come through with nothing flagged; careful single words do better.

Limits: it judges stress only in single words, and not intonation (a question's rise), and a phone recogniser can mishear, so tips are guidance rather than a verdict. Practice is unscored, so attempts never touch the review schedule.

Tests: `src/screens/speak.test.ts` and `src/screens/practice.speak.test.ts` play the mode through in a simulated DOM with a scripted microphone and model, covering the download offer, recording feedback, "I'm done", results and tips, and microphone problems. `src/phonetics.test.ts` covers spelling to sounds, comparison, likelihood and tips, and `src/spoken.test.ts` judges every card's clips as Speak would.

## Audio

Every word, example and drill sentence has a recorded clip, so words play the moment they're tapped and sound the same on every device. Every text is recorded in two voices, a woman's and a man's, and each play picks one at random (a slow replay keeps the voice it just heard). The clips are made with OpenAI's conversational voice model (`gpt-audio-1.5`, voices "marin" and "cedar", asked to read each line as a warm, smiling Filipino from Manila), as its text-to-speech model sounded like a newsreader, by `scripts/generate-audio.mjs`, which also trims the silence at each end (to 50 ms before the first sound and 150 ms after the last, measured against each take's own level so a soft "h" or a fading "-an" is kept whole; `scripts/clip-edges.mjs`), evens out loudness, and checks each take by transcribing it back (and by the model's own account of what it said, as a chat model can answer a line instead of reading it). A transcript can't tell how a letter was said, and the voice sometimes reads Tagalog spelling the English way ("Gising" like "giant", where Tagalog g is always hard), so it's told the Tagalog letter sounds, and each take also goes through Speak's sound recogniser: one where a g before e or i comes out soft (`englishReadings` in `src/phonetics.ts`), or that Speak wouldn't pass as its card, is made again, as is a take the voice cut off mid-sound (it sometimes starts a word beginning with a vowel already sounding) or that starts a moment into its first word, so the recogniser doesn't hear its first consonant ("Kailan" heard as "ailan", `lostFirstSound`). `npm run audio -- --check` runs those checks over the clips already made and remakes the ones that fail, along with any made before this trim, which start right on their first sound. The slow version is the same take slowed to 70%, keeping its pitch. `src/spoken.ts` lists everything the app can say; after adding or changing cards, run `OPENAI_API_KEY=sk-... npm run audio` (it needs ffmpeg) to make the missing clips, and `npm run audio -- --redo "Text"` to remake one that sounds wrong. What the recogniser heard in every clip is kept in `src/clip-sounds.json` (a run listens to any clip not in there, with no key needed); `src/spoken.test.ts` fails while any text is missing its clip, or any card's clip wouldn't pass Speak.

Clips are listed in `src/clips.json` by the text they say, one name per voice (`<name>.mp3` and `<name>-slow.mp3`, about 7.5 MB in all). A name ends in a hash of its sound, so a remade take gets a new name. A few seconds after the app opens, it keeps every clip on the device in a cache of its own (`src/clip-cache.ts`), fetching only the ones it doesn't have yet and dropping ones no longer used; the service worker serves them from there. They play through one shared Web Audio context (`src/audio-engine.ts`), as do the results screen's sounds and slices of the learner's recording. Study loads each card's clip as it comes up and Drills load the whole round at the start, decoding them offline so that showing a card doesn't take the audio session (and pause the learner's music); a tap only has to start a buffer.

iOS changes the hardware rate when the microphone opens or closes, and a context made before that can play distorted, so the shared context is replaced once the microphone has opened or closed, and the recorder keeps its own. If the context can't start (iOS only starts audio from a tap), the built-in voice reads the word instead, and a context iOS left interrupted (after a call or Siri) is replaced on the next tap. The audio session is set to playback, so on iOS 17 and later clips play with the phone on silent; earlier versions, and the built-in voice, follow the silent switch. A running context holds the phone's audio (it shows in the Dynamic Island and keeps other apps' audio paused), so it's suspended 30 seconds after the last sound. When the app goes into the background the context is closed, as iOS can hand one back that says it's running but plays nothing, and the next tap makes a new one. An audio button is lit while its word plays.

The built-in voice (browser speech synthesis) is only a fallback, for a browser without Web Audio or a clip that can't load. It uses a Filipino (`fil`/`tl`) voice when the device has one, otherwise a voice with similar spelling-to-sound rules (Indonesian, Malay, then Spanish). Settings' "Test voice" says how long the word took to start, for checking lag on a device.

## Development

```sh
npm install
npm run dev      # local dev server
npm test         # unit tests (scheduler, sessions, saved progress, deck checks)
npm run build    # type-check and build to dist/
```

## Deploying

`.github/workflows/ci.yml` tests and builds every pull request and push, and publishes to GitHub Pages on every push to `main` once its tests pass (Pages is enabled with **Settings › Pages › Source: GitHub Actions**). The build uses relative paths, so it also works from any static host.
